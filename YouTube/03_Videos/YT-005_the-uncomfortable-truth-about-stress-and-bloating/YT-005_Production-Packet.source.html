Section 1

The Script

Length: 17 to 19 min  ·  Format: solo to camera  ·  One idea: your bloating can be driven by your nervous system, not your food.
Delivery: conversational, warm, direct. Talk to one person, not an audience. Pauses are good. The small side-notes and colored labels are for editing, do not read them aloud.
0:00 – 0:35Hook

You cleaned up your diet. You cut the gluten, the dairy, the sugar. You are doing everything the internet told you to do. And you are still bloated by two in the afternoon, so bloated you have unbuttoned your pants at your desk more times than you want to admit.

Here is the uncomfortable truth nobody is telling you. For a lot of women, the bloating was never really about the food. It is about the system that is supposed to be digesting the food, and that system is stuck in the wrong gear.

On screen: The food isn't the problem

In the next few minutes I am going to show you how stress quietly changes your digestion, the signs that your bloating is coming from your nervous system and not your plate, and the simplest place to actually start. Stay with me, because the sign most people miss is the one I am saving for the end.

I am Alina Nazari, I am a Functional Diagnostic Nutrition Practitioner, and I spend my days with women who have been told their labs are normal and that they just need to manage their stress better. Which is a little ironic, because today I am going to tell you that the stress part is real, but "just manage it" is terrible advice when nobody explains what stress is actually doing inside your gut.

So let me explain it the way I wish someone had explained it to me. No jargon. Just the thing nobody connected for you.

Visual  Warm, direct to camera. Lower third with name and FDN-P appears here.
1:15 – 3:00The problem you're stuck in

Here is the frustrating loop so many of you are living in. You notice the bloating. You assume it is a food. So you start subtracting foods. First it is gluten. Then dairy. Then you are down to nine safe foods and you are still bloated, and now you are also anxious every time you eat, because eating has become a math problem.

And the worst part is what it does to your head. You start to feel like your body is broken, or like you are being dramatic, because the bloodwork came back fine and everyone keeps saying you look healthy. You are not broken. You are not dramatic. You have just been handed the wrong map.

On screen: You're not broken. You have the wrong map.

Because here is what that map leaves out completely. Your gut does not run itself. It takes its orders from your nervous system. And if your nervous system is running in emergency mode all day, your digestion is running in emergency mode all day. That is the piece nobody put in front of you. Let me show you exactly how that works.

3:00 – 6:00Point 1 · Your gut has two gears

Your nervous system has two main settings. One is often called rest and digest. The other is fight or flight. And they are almost exactly what they sound like.

When you are in rest and digest, your body feels safe. Blood flows to your stomach and intestines. You make stomach acid. You make digestive enzymes. The muscles of your gut do this slow, coordinated wave that moves food along and sweeps things clean between meals. Digestion is not a light switch, it is an orchestra, and this is the setting where the orchestra actually plays in time.

Now flip to fight or flight. Your body thinks there is a threat. It does not matter if the threat is a bear or a full inbox, your body cannot tell the difference. So it does something smart for an emergency and terrible for digestion. It pulls resources away from your gut, because digesting lunch is not the priority when you are supposedly running from a bear. Stomach acid drops. Enzymes drop. That clean-up wave between meals slows down or stalls.

This is the analogy I give every client, and it is the one I want you to remember. Think of your digestion like a car engine, and the timing is off. Every part is there. Every part still fires. But they are firing in the wrong sequence, so instead of running smooth, the whole thing sputters, and food that should move through you smoothly just sits there, fermenting, producing gas.

On screen: Same engine. Wrong timing.

"The food is fine. The system processing it isn't."

Key line  Say this one slowly, it is the heart of the video and pays off the hook. B-roll: simple hand-drawn engine with mistimed pistons (add in post).
6:00 – 9:30Point 2 · Why "eating cleaner" backfires

Now here is the trap, and I need you to really hear this one, because it is the thing that keeps smart, disciplined women stuck for years.

When your digestion is stuck in the wrong gear and you bloat, your instinct is to eat cleaner and eat less. But think about what that actually does. Stress already lowered your stomach acid and your enzymes. Then you start eating a giant raw salad and a pile of raw vegetables, because that is what "clean" looks like, and raw, high-fiber food is some of the hardest food to break down.

So now you have a weak digestive setup trying to process the most demanding possible meal, in a body that still feels unsafe. That is not a diet problem you can subtract your way out of. That is a timing problem. And no amount of removing foods fixes the timing.

This is why you can do a perfect elimination diet and still bloat. It is why the bloating seems to have a mind of its own, worse on your most stressful days, weirdly fine on vacation on day four when your shoulders finally drop. You have probably noticed that. Bloating that is calm on vacation and loud on Monday is not a food pattern. It is a nervous system pattern.

On screen: Calm on vacation. Loud on Monday.

9:30 – 12:30Point 3 · What chronic stress does over time

So far I have described what happens in a stressful moment. But most of you are not dealing with a moment. You are dealing with years. And that changes things.

When your body sits in that low-grade emergency mode for months and years, a few things tend to stack up. That clean-up wave between meals, the one that sweeps your small intestine clean, it keeps getting interrupted, so things that should keep moving start to linger and settle where they should not. Your body is producing less of the acid and enzymes that would normally keep everything in check. And your gut lining, which is doing a lot of quiet work at the best of times, is trying to do that work in a body that keeps telling it there is an emergency.

I am not going to name a condition here, because you do not need a scary label, you need the pattern. And the pattern is this. It is rarely one dramatic thing. It is a system that has been asked to digest under pressure for so long that it forgot how to shift back into the calm gear on its own. That is what I call the Wired-and-Tired pattern. Wired, because your system is revved up. Tired, because running an engine in the wrong gear all day is exhausting, and I bet you are exhausted.

On screen: Wired. And tired.
Tone  Reassuring here, not scary. Do not fear-monger.
12:30 – 15:00Point 4 · The signs it's stress-driven

So how do you know if this is you? Here are the signs I look for, and this is the part I asked you to stay for.

One. Your bloating is worse in the afternoon and evening, and better first thing in the morning before the day gets to you.

Two. It tracks your stress more than any single food. Big deadline week, worse. Slow weekend, better.

Three. You eat fast, standing up, between meetings, without really tasting it. Eating in a rush keeps you in the wrong gear before the food even lands.

Four. You are tired but wired. You crash in the afternoon, then get a second wind at night when you finally want to sleep.

And here is the fifth one, the sign almost everyone misses. You feel better when you eat cooked, warm, simple food, and worse when you eat raw and cold and complicated, even when the raw food is technically "healthier." If a warm bowl of soup sits better than a big raw salad, your body is telling you the issue is capacity, not the food. That one sign reframes everything.

On screen: Warm soup over raw salad? That's a clue.
Payoff  This closes the loop you opened in the hook. On-screen checklist builds one line at a time.
15:00 – 16:45Point 5 · Where to actually start

So what do you do with all this? Not another elimination diet, I promise. You start by changing the gear you eat in, before you ever change what is on the plate.

The simplest, most underrated thing you can do is this. Before you eat, take sixty seconds. Sit down, actually sit, put your fork down, and take five slow breaths where the exhale is longer than the inhale. That long exhale is a direct signal to your nervous system that you are safe, and it nudges you toward the digest gear before the first bite. It sounds too simple to matter. It is not. You are not adding a supplement, you are changing the setting the whole meal runs in.

Then, favor warm, cooked, simple meals while your system relearns the calm gear, especially earlier in the day. You are not doing this forever. You are giving a tired system an easier job while it recovers.

None of this is about willpower, and none of it is about you being broken. It is about giving the orchestra a chance to play in time again.

Section 4

Shoot Day Checklist & Upload Copy

For Steve. Everything to film it and upload it.

1. Before the shoot

  • Alina reads the script out loud twice. Familiarity, not memorization.
  • Lock the 3 word-for-word lines: the hook, the aha line ("The food is fine. The system processing it isn't."), and the close ("You're not broken. Your timing is just off.").
  • Wardrobe: solid warm tones (cream, sage, terracotta, navy). No busy patterns, no pure white or black, no big logos.
  • Location: quiet room, soft daylight or a key light, warm non-clinical background.
  • Water nearby, phone silent, notifications off.

2. Gear & setup

  • Camera at eye level, framed chest-up, eyes on the top third.
  • Light in front and slightly to one side, softened. Avoid overhead-only light.
  • Audio is the priority: lav or shotgun mic close to her, not the built-in mic. Record 10 seconds of room tone.
  • Teleprompter app just under or over the lens so her eyeline stays on camera. Slow scroll.
  • Do a 20-second test: focus, exposure, audio levels.

3. Delivery notes for Alina

  • Talk to one person, not an audience.
  • Slow down on the hook and the aha line. Pauses read as confidence.
  • Energy slightly higher than feels natural (camera flattens it ~20%).
  • Fine to restart a sentence: pause, clap once (helps the editor), redo the line.
  • Warm and direct, never clinical. Say stiff lines the way she would to a client.

4. Shot list

A-roll: film each section as its own take: Hook, Credential, Problem, Points 1 to 5, Bridge/CTA, Outro.

B-roll (10 to 20 sec each, silent):

  • Woman at a desk unbuttoning a waistband, hand on stomach (hook).
  • Fridge with "safe food" lists, a symptom journal (problem).
  • Alina demonstrating the sit-down, fork-down, slow exhale (Point 5, also a clip).
  • A warm bowl of soup next to a big raw salad (Point 4).
  • Optional cozy calm cues: tea, candle, slow calming gesture.
